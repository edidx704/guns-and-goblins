The Opaline lamp hissed in the corner, soft and irregular, like breathing.

Mira had turned it low—heavy-grade fuel wasn't cheap anymore, not since the convoys started running through. She'd mentioned the price when he arrived, doubling in three days, in that way people do when they need to fill silence with something ordinary.

The tea steamed in his cup. Irisol saccharide crystals dissolved slowly, three stirs of the spoon. Habit, not measurement. Her hands knew the ritual without watching.

Outside, pecos shifted in the yard. Low clucking, the scrape of claws on packed dirt. One pecked at something—repetitive, patient—sound bleeding through the walls. The world continuing as if nothing had changed.

The waystation was two main rooms, a yard out back, a stable for the birds. She'd run it for decades, she said. Her son had helped. Three days ago, he'd been in the street with the militia when the Moranians came through.

She hadn't said more than that. She'd just made tea.

There was an Autoclast in the yard, hauling firewood. The little humanoid machine moved with the careful deliberation of something that didn't quite understand what it was for. It set a stack of wood near the door, adjusted, knocked the pile over with a mechanical clatter, and made a soft clicking sound—registering an error.

Mira didn't look up.

"Your—"

"He's fine." She poured more tea. "Means well."

The Autoclast tried again. Wood tumbled. Click.

"He's not very smart," she said.

The defector—Venn, he'd told her, just Venn—sat in the chair by the window. Pointy ears, light violet complexion, his coat was dust-stained, his boots worn. Three days of walking from the border. He kept his left hand in his pocket. Just once, adjusting his weight, the fabric pulled tight over something hard beneath.

The lamp hissed.

"You're not from around here." Not a question.

"Passing through."

"They're all passing through now. Moran convoys. Refugees running from the east." She looked at him then—eyes sharp beneath white hair, a face that had weathered decades of ordinary life and now this. "You're not a refugee."

"No."

"And you're not Moran. I've seen them. You don't walk like them."

He didn't answer. The tea was warm in his hands. He hadn't had tea in weeks. The Moran camps ran on coffee, strong and bitter, drunk by officers who needed to stay awake through long nights of processing.

"The price of everything's gone up." She picked up a cloth, wiped the table in slow circles. "Opaline. Honey. Grain. They ship the good stuff back to Morania and leave us the scraps."

The Autoclast outside finally managed to stack the wood. It stood there for a moment, as if surprised by its own success, then turned and walked back toward the woodpile—earnest, mechanical, almost endearing.

"There's a boy here," Venn said.

"My grandson. Tam." She paused. "His father was my son."

"I'm sorry."

"Everyone's sorry. Doesn't change anything." She refilled his cup without asking. "Where are you headed?"

"Away."

"From what?"

He looked at her. The Opaline lamp threw shadows across her face. She was tired—the kind that came from grief, not just age. But she was still here. Still making tea.

"From the war," he said.

She nodded. Didn't ask more. Just made tea, and he drank it, and outside the pecos clucked softly in the yard while the Autoclast knocked over another pile of wood.

The boy appeared at dusk—small, eight or nine, brown hair and the pointed ears of a Central Elm. He stood in the doorway, watching Venn with the particular intensity of a child who had learned that adults were sources of information.

"Grandmother, who's this?"

"A guest. Mind your manners."

"I'm Tam." He came closer, unafraid. "Are you a soldier?"

"No."

"You look like a soldier."

"I'm not."

Tam studied him with the frank assessment children give to things they don't understand. "Your coat has buttons like the Moran soldiers. And your boots have that clicky sound when you walk. Like theirs."

"Tam."

"I'm just looking."

"I used to be," Venn said.

"Used to be what?"

"A soldier."

Tam processed this. "Which side?"

The grandmother made a sound—a warning.

"It's alright." Venn looked at the boy. "Does it matter?"

"The Moran soldiers say the Republic are monkeys. The Republic soldiers say Moran are monsters." Tam tilted his head. "But the Goblin soldiers say both sides use them like kindling. So which kindling-starter are you?"

The question landed differently than Venn expected. Not moral complexity—something stranger. A child trying to map the world through the fragments he'd overheard.

"Neither," Venn said.

"Then what are you?"

The tea was warm in Venn's hands. The saccharide had dissolved completely now, sweetness settled into the bottom of the cup. He thought about the papers in his pocket—forged, but good. He thought about the name he'd given. Not his real name. Not even his cover name. Just a name.

"Someone passing through," he said.

Tam sat down across from him. The grandmother watched but didn't interrupt. She was refilling the kettle, the ritual continuing around them like water flowing past stones.

"Are you one of them? A Mordari?"

Venn didn't answer.

"The soldiers say Mordari are all dark and beautiful and eat babies." Tam's face showed genuine confusion. "But you don't look like you eat babies. You look like you eat... nothing."

The grandmother set the kettle down harder than necessary. "Tam."

"I'm just asking! How else will I know?"

"They say a lot of things." Venn's voice was quiet. "Doesn't make them true."

"One of them called Mr. Henstrum a rat-cattle because he's short." Tam's brow furrowed with the sincere outrage of a child encountering injustice for the first time. "He's not even a Halfling—he's just short. He makes good bread. Why would they say that?"

"Because they can." The grandmother's voice was tired. "Because it makes them feel big to make others small." She looked at Venn. "This is what they've brought us. Three days, and my grandson knows what it feels like to be called filth."

The kettle began to whistle. The grandmother turned to tend it. Tam stayed where he was, watching Venn with curiosity that had no bottom.

"What's your name?"

"Venn."

"That's not a Mittelland name."

"No."

"Is it Mordari?"

"Maybe."

"Can you say something in Mordari?"

Venn looked at him. The boy wasn't testing him. He just wanted to hear the shape of a different language.

"Lanta seleri," Venn said. *Peace go with you.*

"That sounds like music." Tam seemed pleased. "What does it mean?"

"It means 'goodbye.'"

"But you're not leaving yet."

"No."

Tam nodded, accepting this. Children moved through conversations strangely, following tracks adults couldn't see.

"Are you going to stay?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I can't."

Tam considered this. "Is it because you did something bad?"

The question was too direct. Venn looked at the boy—at the earnest face, the pointed ears, the brown hair that probably came from his father—and felt the weight of the last three weeks settle heavier on his shoulders.

"Everyone's done something bad," Venn said.

"Not Grandmother. She just makes tea and feeds birds and yells at the Autoclast when it knocks things over."

"Your grandmother is braver than most."

Tam nodded as if this was obvious information. "She made tea for the Moran soldiers yesterday. They didn't even say thank you. They just took it and left." He paused. "Do you want to see something?"

Before Venn could answer, Tam had slipped off his chair and run to the back room. He returned holding a small wooden figure—crude, carved by an unskilled hand, the shape of a peco.

"Father made this." He held it out. "Before."

Venn took the figure. It was rough but beloved—the kind of object a child treasures because someone made it for them. He turned it over in his hands.

"He was good at carving," Tam said. "He was going to make me a whole flock. One for each real peco." He looked toward the window, where the birds had gone quiet with the fading light. "There's six pecos. He only made three. I don't know if I should keep them with the others or keep them special."

"Keep them however feels right," Venn said. "There's no wrong answer."

Tam took the figure back, studying it as if Venn had given him a puzzle to solve. Then he tucked it into his pocket.

"I'm going to show you the birds tomorrow. They're funny. One of them pecks at the Autoclast sometimes." He stood. "Are you going to sleep? Grandmother says I have to let guests sleep."

"I'll sleep soon."

Tam went to the back room. At the doorway he stopped and turned.

"Lanta seleri," he said, mangling the pronunciation but getting all the syllables.

"Lanta seleri," Venn replied.

The boy disappeared into the back. Venn heard him say something to his grandmother—too quiet to make out—and her low response, the murmur of ordinary comfort.

He looked at the wooden peco he'd held. A dead man's gift to his son. Three of six, never to be finished.

The Autoclast outside made a soft mechanical sound, like a sigh, and settled into its rest cycle.

He dreamed of a form.

Not a nightmare—not exactly. Just a memory that wouldn't stay buried.

He saw it clearly. The seal at the top. The Moran Imperial crest. The columns of data. Unit designations, deployment zones, projected casualty rates. And at the bottom, the space for his signature.

*Kade ven Istar, Third Bureau, Administrative Section.*

He remembered the weight of the pen. The scratch of ink on paper. The little voice in the back of his head saying *this is just procedure.*

**Unit Designation: Goblin Auxiliaries 7th Forward Reconnaissance. Personnel Count: 127. Deployment: Aris Ridge Approach, Sector 8. Mission Profile: Draw enemy fire, identify emplacements. Expected Survivors: 0-3.**

One hundred and twenty-seven. Not people. Numbers.

In his dream, the numbers had faces. They came out of the paper, crawling over the form, pulling themselves into existence. Green skin, sharp teeth, black hair plastered with sweat. They looked at him with eyes that held no judgment. Only exhaustion.

*You signed,* they said. *You signed and we went.*

And at the front of them—always at the front—a goblin woman with red hair and a birthmark on her left cheek. She held something in her hands. A letter, maybe. Something she'd been writing home.

*You don't even know my name,* she said.

He woke to the Opaline lamp hissing and the grey light of pre-dawn seeping through the shutters.

He'd been signing forms for two years before he'd learned her name, found it by accident in a casualty report: Kessa Welbruk. Seventeen years old. Had a mother in Estria, in a village called Aurbruch, near the border.

He'd looked it up. Two days' walk from here.

The form with her name on it was folded in his pocket. He didn't know why he'd kept it. Evidence, maybe. Or penance.

---

They came at dawn.

The pecos heard them first—a burst of panicked clucking from the yard, the scrape of claws on packed dirt as the birds scattered. Then the engines: low rumble of Opaline motors, heavy fuel.

The grandmother's hand paused over the kettle.

Venn stayed in his chair. Hand in pocket. Shadow in the corner.

The vehicles stopped. Doors opened. Laughter—loud, careless, the sound of men who owned the space they were entering.

Two soldiers pushed through the door. The first was young, Central Elm, barely twenty. His uniform was too new, his eyes too wide. He held his rifle like he wasn't sure what it was for.

The second was older. Mordari features—blue-tinged skin, white hair, violet eyes that swept the room with tired contempt. His uniform was worn, his posture loose. He'd been doing this a long time.

"Papers," the Mordari soldier said. His Mittelland was perfect. "Everyone."

The grandmother set down the kettle. Her hands were steady.

"I am Mira Valden. This is my grandson, Tam. We run this waystation."

"Papers."

She produced them from a drawer. The young soldier took them, fumbled through the pages. The Mordari soldier wasn't watching—he was looking at the kitchen, the lamp, the kettle still steaming on the counter.

"Opaline lamp," he said. "Expensive these days, isn't it."

"The convoys use most of it." The grandmother's voice was flat. "We make do."

"Do you." He picked up the kettle, poured himself a cup without asking. Sipped. Made a face. "Irisol sugar. Of course." He set the cup down. "Can't even afford honey."

"We had real honey." The grandmother's voice was still flat. "The Moran took it."

The Mordari soldier's eyes went to her face. Something flickered there—not anger, exactly. Amusement.

"Did they."

"Three days ago. When they came through and shot my son in the street."

The young soldier shifted uncomfortably. The Mordari soldier just sipped his tea.

"Militia," he said. "Casualties happen in occupation zones."

"He was a carpenter."

"He was holding a rifle."

"He was standing in the street." The grandmother's voice was sharper now. "With other men who thought they could protect something. They were wrong. You killed them anyway."

The Mordari soldier set down his cup. His violet eyes were cold.

"You're very outspoken for an old woman in an occupied waystation."

"I'm very tired." She didn't look away. "And you're drinking my tea."

"You think because I'm sitting in your kitchen, drinking your swill, that you can speak to me however you like?"

"I think because you're sitting in my kitchen, drinking my swill, that you're not here on official business." The grandmother's voice was steady. "Official patrols check papers and leave. They don't pour their own tea and insult the Sugar."

The Mordari soldier stood. Slowly. His hand went to his sidearm. The young soldier had gone pale, was watching like he wanted to be anywhere else.

"You're very brave," the Mordari soldier said. "Or very stupid. I haven't decided which." He leaned in. "Let me help you decide. The next time I come through here—"

"You won't."

The voice came from the corner. Quiet. Calm. The first words Venn had spoken since they'd entered.

The Mordari soldier turned.

Venn was standing now, his hand still in his pocket.

"Excuse me?"

"Valdir Morandis." Venn stepped forward, just one step. "Third Bureau, Compliance Section."

The Mordari soldier's eyes narrowed.

"Compliance officers don't travel alone."

"I'm not traveling. I'm inspecting." Venn's voice was still calm. "This waystation is registered under Contract M-447, Moran logistical corps. Any further inspection requires authorization from my office."

"There is no Contract M-447."

"Are you certain?" Venn tilted his head slightly. "The Third Bureau files thousands of contracts. Supply surveys. Infrastructure assessments. Compliance audits." He paused. "You know what a compliance audit looks like, don't you, Sergeant? The kind where they check your requisition forms? Your casualty reports? Make sure the numbers match?"

The Mordari soldier's face went still.

"I don't know what you think—"

"I think you're a long way from your patrol route, soldier. I think you're drinking tea in a waystation that isn't on your rotation." Venn's voice was still quiet. Still calm. "I think if anyone checked your paperwork, they'd find some very interesting discrepancies. And I think when I file my report, those discrepancies will land on someone's desk in Morania."

The young soldier looked between them, confused. The Mordari soldier's hand had tightened on his sidearm.

The Mordari soldier held his gaze for a long moment. Then he turned, gestured to the young soldier, and walked out. The engines started. The vehicles rumbled away.

The grandmother let out a breath.

Tam was still pressed against the wall, eyes wide.

Venn sat back down. His hand was still in his pocket.

He looked at the grandmother's tea. Still steaming. Still warm.

She'd made it for a stranger. Asked nothing. Given him a place to sit, a moment of peace in a war he'd helped create.

The kitchen was quiet. The Opaline lamp hissed. Tam had gone back to bed, but the grandmother stayed, sitting across from Venn, watching him with eyes that had seen too much.

"You didn't have to do that," she said.

Venn looked at the cold tea in his cup. The saccharide had settled at the bottom, making the last sip too sweet.

He reached into his pocket and pulled out the folded form.

"I want to show you something."

He set it on the table. The grandmother looked at it without touching it.

"What is this?"

"An order." Venn's voice was flat. "One of thousands. This one is for a Goblin reconnaissance unit. One hundred and twenty-seven soldiers. They were sent to draw fire at Aris Ridge."

The grandmother's face was unreadable.

"Expected survivors," Venn said, pointing to the line. "Zero to three."

He turned the form over. On the back, in his own handwriting, was a single name.

Kessa Welbruk.

"I found her name in a casualty report. Seventeen years old. Her mother lives in Aurbruch." He looked at the grandmother. "I was going to go there. After. Tell her mother... something. I don't know what."

The grandmother was silent for a long moment. Then she reached out and picked up the form. She studied it—the official seal, the columns of numbers, the name written on the back.

"You signed this?"

"Yes."

"And one hundred and twenty-seven people died because of it."

"Yes."

Tam had crept closer. The boy was listening, his young face serious. He looked at the form, at the name, at Venn.

"Kessa," Tam said quietly. "That's a nice name."

Venn looked at him. At the earnestness in his face, the simple acknowledgment of a name. Not numbers. Not "acceptable losses." A name.

"Yes," Venn said. "It is."

The grandmother set the form down. Her face was still unreadable, but her hands had stopped trembling.

"My son wasn't a soldier," she said. "He was a carpenter. He joined the militia because he thought he should. And now he's dead, and the Moran walk through my kitchen like they own it."

"They do own it. That's what occupation means."

"Why are you here?" The grandmother's voice cracked, just slightly. "Why are you in my kitchen, drinking my tea, using your presence to make soldiers nervous? What do you want?"

Venn was quiet for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Outside, a peco made a low, mournful sound.

"I don't know," he said finally. "I was running. I thought I'd go to Aurbruch. Tell a mother that her daughter—" He stopped. "I don't know what I thought. That saying something would make it better. That it would mean something."

"It wouldn't."

"No. It wouldn't."

The grandmother studied him. Her sharp eyes missed nothing—not the way he held his left arm, not the quality of his coat beneath the dust, not the form on the table with its death count and single name.

"You're Mordari," she said. "The Moran killed my son. But you didn't. You're sitting here with empty hands, drinking tea I made because that's what you do when someone comes to your door." She picked up the kettle. "You want forgiveness? I can't give you that. But I can give you tea. And that's something."

She poured. He drank. And outside, the Autoclast knocked over another pile of wood.

Night fell. The grandmother turned the Opaline lamp low. The hiss grew softer, almost peaceful, like a sleeping creature.

Tam was asleep in the back room—Venn could hear the boy's breathing through the thin walls, steady and untroubled. Sleep came easily to children. The world hadn't weighed them down yet.

Venn sat by the window, watching the road. The darkness was complete—no village lights, no lanterns from travelers. The Moran had imposed a curfew, the grandmother had told him. Anyone outside after dark was assumed resistance. The assumption was usually correct, she'd added, which was why she'd closed the shutters.

They would come back. He knew it. The corporal had noticed something in the way he stood. And sooner or later, he'd mention it to someone, and someone would check, and there was no Contract M-447.

And when they found out—

Movement. At the edge of the yard. Not a soldier's gait—but someone who knew how to move quietly.

A figure emerged from the darkness. A woman—Central Elm, wavy blonde hair and soft Elm features. She wore civilian clothes, but she moved like someone who'd been trained. Militia. Or what was left of it.

"Sera." She stayed at the edge of the lamplight. "Estrian resistance. I've been watching the waystation. Saw the patrol leave. They took their time."

"They were thorough."

She moved closer, and he noticed details he'd missed before. Her hands were rough—worker's hands, not a soldier's. There was a callus on her left thumb from a needle. Embroidery, maybe. Or tailoring.

"The occupation is three days old," she said. "The King is dead—an 'accident.' The Princess is missing, nobody knows where she is. The Moran just walked in and took everything." Her voice was flat. "We weren't ready. Some of us are trying to change that."

Venn didn't respond.

"You helped that woman. Made yourself visible doing it." She gestured toward the back room. "Come with us. We can use someone who knows their language."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you keep running. And they catch you anyway, eventually." She paused. "Or you fight."

Venn looked toward the back room. The grandmother was sleeping. Tam was sleeping. They'd given him tea and asked nothing. The grandmother had lost her son, and she'd still made space for a stranger.

"They'll come back here," Venn said. "When they figure it out. This waystation—"

"We know. We'll move them." Sera stepped closer. "You made a choice when you helped her. You can't unmake it. The question is what you do next."

Before Venn could answer, Sera tensed. Her head turned toward the road.

"They're coming." Her voice dropped. "Faster than I thought."

---

The vehicles came with headlights blazing—three trucks, moving fast. Venn saw them through the window and knew the bluff had run its course.

"Get them out," he said to Sera. "The grandmother. The boy."

"There's no time—"

"Get them out."

Sera hesitated, then moved. She slipped into the back room. Venn heard her wake the grandmother, low urgent tones. He stayed by the window, watching the trucks stop. Soldiers piling out. Six—no, seven. Four Elm. Two Goblins. And one in an officer's coat.

The grandmother emerged, Tam clinging to her hand. Sera behind them, rifle slung over her shoulder.

"Go," Venn said. "Out the back."

"You can't—" the grandmother started.

"Go."

She looked at him. At the hand still in his pocket. She took Tam and ran.

The door opened.

The officer entered first. Older than the patrol soldiers, thinner, with the careful eyes of someone who processed information rather than bodies.

"Compliance Officer Valdir Morandis." His Mittelland was precise, almost perfect. "Contract M-447."

Venn didn't move.

"There is no Contract M-447." The officer smiled thinly. "Your script is Court Mordarin, but the seal is... imaginative."

The soldiers behind him raised their rifles.

"You're not a Compliance Officer. You're Kade ven Istar. Third Bureau. Administrative Section. Deserted a week ago from the front." The smile widened. "A Mordari bureaucrat. The Scholar Council wants you back. They have questions about certain documents you took."

Venn's left hand came out of his pocket.

He pulled off the glove.

The Automail prosthetic gleamed in the lamplight.

The officer's eyes went to the arm. "Ah."

"Your men should leave," Venn said.

"They're Moran soldiers. They don't leave."

The overclocker flared.

The plasma blade erupted from his wrist, a column of hissing blue-white fire that blasted outward like a captured star set free. The light was blinding, filling the small kitchen with brilliance so pure that shadows ceased to exist. Every face frozen in shock—illuminated in terrible clarity.

Venn was already moving.

The first soldier died before he could raise his rifle. The plasma blade caught him center-mass and didn't slow—It cut through armor like paper, through flesh, through bone. Octarine plasma burned at temperatures that didn't care about breastplates. The man came apart in the light, top half sliding one direction, bottom half another, steaming edges the only proof he'd been whole a moment before.

The second soldier managed to fire. A wild shot that shattered the Opaline lamp. But Venn didn't need light. The arm blazed like a sun, and his blade was already carving through the air.

The third soldier screamed. Venn pivoted, plasma trailing arcs of superheated gas, and bisected him at the waist. The scream cut off. Two halves of a man hit the floor in two different places.

More shots from outside. One hit his side—a hammer blow that knocked the breath from him. Fifty seconds left on the blade. Maybe forty.

Outside, Sera's rifle cracked. One of the soldiers in the yard dropped.

The Goblins—they were armed, they were Moran soldiers, they were doing their job. One of them had red hair.

Thirty seconds.

And then his plasma blade cut through the Goblin before he could think, before he could remember Kessa from Aurbruch, before he could—

Twenty seconds.

The officer was running. Venn let him go. No point chasing.

Ten seconds.

He positioned himself near the door, blade still blazing, covering Sera's extraction.

The blade guttered. Sputtered. Died.

The prosthesis glowed—not the clean blue-white of volatile octarine, but orange-red, the color of metal held in a forge. Heat radiated from it in waves. Venn's coat sleeve began to smoke where it touched the arm. The smell of burning fabric filled the kitchen.

The metal fingers were locked, joints seized from thermal stress. The arm would stay like this for a while—glowing, dangerous, useless.

He backed away from the bodies. The grandmother's kitchen was a ruin—blood, bullet holes, the acrid stench of plasma discharge mixing with cooked meat.

On the floor, the Goblin soldier's face was half-visible in the dim light. Red hair, matted with blood.

Venn looked away.

Sera appeared in the doorway. She stopped, feeling the heat from across the room.

"Your arm—"

"I know." He was breathing hard. The wound in his side bled freely. His left arm hung at his side, glowing like a warning beacon, radiating heat that made the air shimmer. "Needs to cool. Don't touch it."

"They'll send more."

"Then we move. Now. While I can still walk."

He looked at the bodies. The Goblins. The Elm. All dead because he'd drunk tea and helped an old woman and pretended he could be something other than what he was.

"Come on." Sera's voice was tight. "The grandmother and boy are safe. We need to move."

Venn nodded. He looked around the ruined kitchen—at the shattered lamp, the overturned kettle, the tea spilled on the floor—and followed her into the night.

---

Dawn came gray and cold.

Venn walked most of the night, Sera guiding him through fields and forest paths that avoided the main roads. His side ached where the bullet had grazed him. His left arm had finally stopped glowing an hour after the fight, but the prosthesis was damaged—the Oktarin blade had burned it from the inside. The hand didn't respond properly. Fingers twitched, seized, refused to grip. He'd need a mechanic. Oktarin technology wasn't something you fixed yourself.

They reached the resistance safe house as the sun rose—a barn on a farm outside the village. The grandmother and Tam were already there, wrapped in blankets, drinking something hot from tin cups. The smell—some kind of grain broth—reminded Venn that he couldn't remember the last time he'd eaten.

When Venn entered, Tam ran to him.

"You're hurt!"

"I'm fine." His voice was rough. His side was bandaged, but the wound ached. His left arm hung stiff, the hand barely functional. "Are you alright?"

"Grandmother's sad. But she says we're safe now."

Venn looked at the grandmother. She met his eyes across the barn. She'd lost her waystation—her home of decades—because she'd made tea for a stranger.

He went to her.

"I'm sorry," he said. "Your home—"

"Was already gone." Her voice was steady. "The Moran took everything three days ago. My son. My peace. My country." She looked at him. "My grandson gets to live. That's worth a waystation."

Venn didn't know what to say.

The grandmother reached into her pocket and produced a small cloth bundle.

"Tea," she said. "For the road. Not much, but..." She shrugged. "Drink it. Remember us."

Venn took the bundle. It was warm from her pocket.

Sera appeared at the door. "We need to move. Moran will have the roads blocked by noon."

Venn nodded. He looked at Tam one more time.

"You're like the stories," Tam said.

Venn managed something that might have been a smile. "Don't believe all the stories."

"I won't." Tam waved. "Goodbye, Venn."

"Lanta seleri," Venn said.

Tam's face lit up. "Lanta seleri!" The pronunciation was still wrong, but the intent was clear.

Venn left with Sera, the barn disappearing into morning mist behind him. Somewhere, the Moran were already mobilizing. Somewhere, forms were being signed and orders processed and bodies counted as resources.

He thought of Kessa Welbruk. Of her mother in Aurbruch, two days' walk from here. Maybe he would go there. Maybe he would say something.

Or maybe he would stay with the resistance. Learn to fight instead of sign.

He touched the cloth bundle in his pocket. Still warm. The grandmother had given him tea, and asked nothing.

That was something.
